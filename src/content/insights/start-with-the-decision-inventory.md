Most AI conversations begin with a list of use cases. There is a chatbot for HR, a forecasting model for inventory, and a tool that summarises meeting notes. Each one sounds reasonable. Put together, they rarely change how the business performs.

A more useful place to begin is a list of the decisions your organisation makes again and again. We call it a decision inventory.

## What goes into a decision inventory

For each decision your business makes weekly, monthly, or many times a day, note four things.

1. **Who makes it.** Write down the role, since people change jobs.
2. **How often it is made.** Include how many times it happens across the organisation.
3. **What information it needs.** Note where that information lives today.
4. **What a poor call costs.** A rough figure in money, time, or customers is enough.

A franchise network might list decisions such as which locations need a visit this week, whether a royalty statement is ready to sign off, or which products to move between stores. None of these sounds like an AI use case. All of them improve when the right information arrives sooner.

## Why it helps

The inventory ties AI to outcomes that leadership already cares about. It also shows where information is late or missing, which is often the real problem. And because it records how often each decision is made and what a mistake costs, it points you to a first project that is likely to pay back.

## How to run one

You can build the first version with your leadership team in a single working session. Begin with the decisions on the agenda of your weekly and monthly reviews, since those tend to matter most. Then ask each function head for the five decisions their team makes most often.

You will probably end up with thirty to fifty decisions. Choose the three that are frequent, expensive to get wrong, and short of timely information. Those are good candidates for your first AI project.
